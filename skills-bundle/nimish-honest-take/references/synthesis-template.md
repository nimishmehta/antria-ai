# Synthesis + value stress test — template & churn loop

The headline deliverable: `<target>-honest-take.md`. It combines both briefs into one first-principles verdict, ranked by pain severity and dollars, and is only finished once it survives the convergence audit. Executive, blunt, value-first.

---

## Structure

### The one-line verdict
Lead with the conclusion. One sentence a busy decision-maker can repeat.

### 1. The problem, from first principles
Strip the product away entirely. What does the buyer fundamentally want? Refine in layers and show the refinement:
- **Layer 1 (surface):** what everyone says they want (usually a cliché — "transparency," "efficiency").
- **Layer 2 (deeper):** what that's really instrumental to.
- **Layer 3+ (bedrock):** the irreducible want — usually some form of *relief + proof* (someone takes the problem off my desk, and proves it's working). Keep going until the statement stops moving.

State the bedrock problem in one sentence. If it only makes sense because the product exists, you retrofitted — restart.

### 2. Buyer pains, ranked by severity × dollars
The master table — the spine of the verdict:

| # | Pain (the job to be done) | Severity (1–5) | $ value attached | Built today? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | (highest severity × $) | | | ✅ / ⚠️ partial / ❌ not built |

Rank from the buyer's world, not the product's feature list. The "Built today?" column is the punchline.

### 3. Map: what's built → which pain
For each real capability, the pain it serves and whether that's a top pain or peripheral. Make the mismatch (if any) impossible to miss.

### 4. The inversion check
State plainly: is the build aimed at the high-severity/high-dollar pains, or at the easy, commoditized, peripheral ones? Most weak products are *inverted* — they nail the cheap jobs and leave the expensive ones unbuilt while marketing the expensive ones. If inverted, say so in one blunt line.

### 5. How many products is this, really?
One / two / N. The redundancies. The honest org chart (spine + modules + orphans/marketing assets).

### 6. The missing engine
The load-bearing, unbuilt pieces that would actually win and retain — ranked by severity × dollars. This is usually the most useful section: it tells the team where the next dollar of effort goes.

### 7. Built vs. wins-the-deal
A two-column contrast — what's built today vs. what actually wins and keeps the account. The clarity device that lands the whole argument.

### 8. The focus call
What to build, buy/bundle, integrate, reposition, or cut — in severity × dollar order. Decisive.

### Bottom line
One paragraph. Is the strategy right? Is the build aimed correctly? What's the single most important move?

---

## Convergence audit (the churn loop)
After drafting the synthesis, run this checklist. **If any item fails, revise and re-run.** Repeat until all pass and the verdict would survive both personas reading it. Note how many passes it took.

- [ ] **No retrofitting.** The problem statement was derived independently of the product. It would still be true if the product didn't exist.
- [ ] **Grounded.** Every value claim traces to a *built* line in the ledger — no credit for mock/stub/spec/roadmap.
- [ ] **Market-ranked.** Pains are ranked by severity × dollars from the buyer's world, not reordered to match what the product happens to do.
- [ ] **Competitors named.** Real alternatives and incumbents are named and honestly compared.
- [ ] **Need vs. nice.** Each capability is graded need-to-have vs. nice-to-have, not lumped together.
- [ ] **Gap stated.** The headline-vs-build gap is stated plainly, not softened.
- [ ] **Consolidation answered.** The "how many products is this" question has a clear number.
- [ ] **Fair.** Genuine assets are credited; the take is accurate, not reflexively negative.
- [ ] **Persona test.** Both the buyer and the seller persona would nod, not call BS.
- [ ] **Decisive.** The focus call is concrete and ordered by severity × dollars — not a list of "could consider."

Only when every box is checked is the honest take done.
